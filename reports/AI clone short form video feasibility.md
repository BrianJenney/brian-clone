# Build Brian's clone on rented engines

Yes, it is feasible, and it is cheaper and simpler than Doxa's marketing makes it look. Doxa's own Terms of Service say the company "relies on ElevenLabs and HeyGen as infrastructure providers," so its "five years of AI" is an orchestration layer on top of two APIs Brian can call directly ([Doxa app ToS](https://app.doxascale.com/login)). A high-quality version for one creator is an ElevenLabs Professional Voice Clone, a HeyGen v3 digital twin, a programmatic editor (Remotion or HyperFrames), and a small trend engine built on third-party scraping APIs. The marginal cost is about **$1–4 per 30–45 second short**, plus under $100/month for trend data. Brian has to film once: about 30 minutes for avatar looks and a consent clip. His existing YouTube and podcast audio covers the voice clone. The "Reverse Engineer Anything" repo is real and popular, but you don't need it here. The useful facts about Doxa's stack are already in its public JS bundles, and the tool can't see server-side pipelines anyway. The real risk isn't technical. Brian sells credibility to developers, the sharpest-eyed audience there is for spotting synthetic video. Use the clone as a volume layer over real footage, label it openly, and keep stories, opinions and confessions on camera. Build it in four phases, and gate each phase on measured retention against Brian's real-face baseline.

## Doxa is a HeyGen + ElevenLabs wrapper sold as a guarantee

Doxa is a Brazilian startup trading as Doxa Inc., with a Palo Alto mailing address and California-law terms. Its sites are doxaviral.com and doxascale.com. Its pitch is "one photo and one audio become sixty pieces of content in ninety days," backed by a "1M views or your money back" headline ([doxaviral.com](https://www.doxaviral.com/)). Founder Diogo Guilhon previously ran the same offer as Spreed.Ai, a long-podcast-to-viral-clips tool. The same 60-videos/1M-views guarantee and a sales video hosted on a "Tech Spreed" YouTube channel tie the two together ([Diário do Comércio](https://diariodocomercio.com.br/tecnologia/mineiro-plataforma-ia-viralizar-videos-fatura-700-mil/); [YouTube VSL](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=V8Y6mQ3jGoU)). Press reports **R$30M in 2025 revenue, ~900 videos/day, 1,500+ clients and 10B+ client views**. All of those are unverified company claims ([The University Journal](https://theuniversityjournal.com/1-milhao-de-visualizacoes-organicas-ou-seu-dinheiro-de-volta-startup-de-ia-co-fundada-por-estudantes-do-insper-fatura-r30-milhoes-e-abre-escritorio-em-miami/)).

The client app's shipped JavaScript spells out the workflow. A client completes a scored business questionnaire, and anything below 75 points blocks progress because it is "the basis for every theme and script the AI will generate." The client then uploads **three photos (frontal, profile, 45°)** that get composited into a "DOXA studio" podcast set, and records **about 5 minutes of voice**. An ElevenLabs Professional Voice Clone trains for a few hours. Staff score each avatar 0–10, and anything under 6 isn't auto-approved. Clips then move through "Generating face → Generating speech (lipsync) → Editing → Ready." Each clip costs 2 credits. Clients must paste back the link of each posted video so "our AI understands the real performance of your content," and editing an AI script voids the guarantee ([Doxa app](https://app.doxascale.com/login)). The admin UI is explicit about the stack: "Fill in the HeyGen Avatar ID and ElevenLabs Voice ID," with manual fallbacks for when HeyGen or ElevenLabs automation fails. The marketing site's logo strip adds OpenAI, Claude and Meta as LLM suppliers ([doxaviral.com](https://www.doxaviral.com/)). Doxa says its trend engine "analyzes more than 100,000 videos per niche" for hooks, structures and retention patterns ([The University Journal](https://theuniversityjournal.com/1-milhao-de-visualizacoes-organicas-ou-seu-dinheiro-de-volta-startup-de-ia-co-fundada-por-estudantes-do-insper-fatura-r30-milhoes-e-abre-escritorio-em-miami/)). It publishes no technical detail about that engine.

"Licensing" doesn't mean likeness licensing. **Licença DOXA is a white-label agency license**: "You don't build the agency. You plug ours into yours." Licensees resell the 1M-views offer and Doxa does the delivery, with one account manager per 100 clients. Doxa claims 60+ licensees and an R$24k average ticket ([Doxa /agency-experience](https://lp.doxascale.com/agency-experience)). Prices aren't published. Sales run through a paid "strategic audit" call ([The Bank checkout](https://checkout.thebank.com.br/pt-BR/7472746024528564224?cc=BR)), and prospects with budgets under R$1,000/month are turned away. The guarantee is engineered to survive. Only the implementation fee is refundable, monthly fees aren't, and clone quality "shall not be used as grounds for refunds." The guarantee is voided by ads, edited scripts, deleted videos, off-niche trending sounds and more ([Doxa app ToS](https://app.doxascale.com/login)). Spread across 36–60 videos, 1M total views is only about 17–28k per video. Complaints against the predecessor Spreed called clone output "shameful, unusable" and alleged refused refunds ([Reclame Aqui](https://www.reclameaqui.com.br/spreed-software-e-producoes-ltda/reclamacao-sobre-servico-de-video-com-ia-da-spreed-qualidade-inferior-ao-prometido-e-nao-conformidade-com-o-contratado_jOTKyFwh9X-u7S6D/)).

For Brian, this means Doxa's moat is high-ticket sales, agency distribution, human QA ops and niche scripting know-how. None of that is model technology. The parts he wants (scripting, voice, avatar, editing, feedback loop) can all be reproduced with direct vendor contracts. The parts he doesn't need, like the guarantee, the agency channel and 1:100 account management, are exactly the parts that are hard to copy.

## REA is a real tool aimed at the wrong layer for this job

"Reverse Engineer Anything" is **morluto/rea**. It is MIT-licensed, has about 415k stars and was pushed the day of this research. It installs as `npx rea-agents setup`, which makes it an MCP server and CLI that hands Claude Code, Codex, Cursor and similar agents roughly 170–207 reverse-engineering tools. Those tools wrap Ghidra, IDA, JADX, Binwalk and TShark, and add their own parsers for JS bundles, Electron, .NET and HAR captures ([GitHub](https://github.com/morluto/rea); [README](https://github.com/morluto/rea/blob/main/README.md)). It is neither a decompiler nor an agent. The LLM does the reasoning ([Next.ink](https://next.ink/261039/rea-comment-un-projet-open-source-se-propose-de-tout-decompiler/)). Install it only from the official repo or npm. Fake "REA download" repos exist, and at least one copy carries a trojan ([codewithmukesh](https://codewithmukesh.com/blog/reverse-engineer-anything-rea/)).

It works, narrowly. The official showcases are single-function reconstructions, such as one DX-Ball routine matching all 63 compiled bytes. The project itself doesn't claim to clone whole applications ([awesome-reverse-engineer-anything](https://github.com/AIshihzong/awesome-reverse-engineer-anything)). An independent Electron test found it fast and honest about its limits. It also missed every preload entrypoint, dropped graph edges for 365 files, and loaded about 500k tokens of tool definitions ([HackerNoon](https://hackernoon.com/i-tested-rea-githubs-viral-reverse-engineering-tool-on-a-real-electron-app)). The most rigorous evaluation ran 482 preregistered Claude Code runs. It found **no significant improvement in solve rate with REA, at about 2x time and cost**. On harder binaries, runs without REA did better: 11/24 solved versus 4/24 with it ([rea-lab](https://github.com/pablodiazjorge/rea-lab)). Nobody has independently benchmarked its browser tools.

On Doxa specifically, REA's web tools (`inspect_web_page`, `analyze_web_bundle`, HAR analysis) would catalogue routes, client API calls, response shapes and bundled SDKs. By its own documentation, it "only observes what the browser sends and receives" ([rea.tools](https://rea.tools/guides/browser/)). Doxa's HeyGen and ElevenLabs calls run server-side, so REA would mostly find Next.js, GA4 and a Brazilian VSL player. The research for this report already pulled more than that from Doxa's public bundles and ToS: vendor names, pipeline states, QA thresholds, credit costs and the feedback loop. **The reverse-engineering is done. Running REA would add cost and legal exposure and no new insight.**

The legal limits are clear enough to act on. Copyright protects code and expression, not functionality. EU law expressly lets a licensee "observe, study or test" a program to find its underlying ideas ([SAS v World Programming](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SAS_Institute_Inc_v_World_Programming_Ltd)). US contract law is stricter. Courts have enforced "no reverse engineering" clauses even where the use would be fair use ([Bowers v. Baystate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bowers_v._Baystate_Technologies,_Inc.)). ToS breaches alone aren't CFAA crimes after *Van Buren*, but LinkedIn still won on breach of contract against hiQ ([Privacy World](https://www.privacyworld.blog/2022/11/federal-court-rules-in-favor-of-linkedins-breach-of-contract-claim-after-six-years-of-cfaa-data-scraping-litigation/)). Doxa's terms grant only "internal business use" and bar reselling outputs "as a competing service" ([Doxa app ToS](https://app.doxascale.com/login)). REA's own disclaimer forbids copying protected code or assets and bypassing access controls ([DISCLAIMER.md](https://github.com/morluto/rea/blob/main/DISCLAIMER.md)). The safe line for Brian: use public pages and a normal trial if he wants one, write the functional spec in his own words, never copy Doxa's UI copy, scripts, studio backgrounds or code, and never call its private APIs. Building on HeyGen and ElevenLabs under his own contracts is unambiguously clean.

## The highest-quality stack costs about $1–4 per short

HeyGen is the most consistently top-ranked tool for cloning a real person. Its v3 API trains a digital twin from 15–600 seconds of footage (about 2 minutes recommended, 1080p, one face in frame, clean audio). It auto-creates a voice and requires a live on-camera consent statement ([HeyGen developer docs](https://developers.heygen.com/docs/avatar-from-video.md)). This is the same engine Doxa uses, but Doxa appears to drive it from photos. Brian should give it real video, which means better motion and likeness than three stills composited into a fake studio. For voice, an ElevenLabs Professional Voice Clone needs 30+ minutes of audio, with 2–3 hours recommended ([invideo](https://invideo.io/blog/ai-voice-model-pricing/)). Brian's YouTube back catalog covers that. Generate audio separately and pass it to HeyGen instead of using HeyGen's auto-voice. That keeps the voice stable across months and returns word-level alignment for captions.

| Layer | Pick | Price | Why / alternative |
|---|---|---|---|
| Avatar (volume) | HeyGen Avatar IV digital twin, API | **$4.83/min** | Mature API; web Creator plan ≈ $1.50/min if rendering manually ([ugcgen.ai](https://ugcgen.ai/heygen-api-pricing)) |
| Avatar (hero clips) | HeyGen Avatar V digital twin | **$7.20/min** | Top realism score (9.2/10 in one review) ([ugcgen.ai](https://ugcgen.ai/heygen-api-pricing); [Venture Harbour](https://ventureharbour.com/best-ai-avatar-software/)) |
| Full-frame generative | OmniHuman 1.5 via fal | $0.16/s (~$9.60/min), 1080p capped at 30 s | Better body motion, pricier ([fal](https://fal.ai/learn/devs/omnihuman-15-user-guide)) |
| Voice | ElevenLabs PVC + Eleven v3 | ~$5.40 per narrated hour | Fish Audio S2 leads identity scores if self-hosting ([invideo](https://invideo.io/blog/ai-voice-model-pricing/)) |
| Lip re-sync on real footage | LatentSync 1.6 (open) / Sync.so | GPU time | Most realistic when real footage exists; known blur issue ([Instavar](https://instavar.com/research/ai-video/open-source-lip-sync-models)) |
| Captions | Own word-timed captions from ElevenLabs alignment | ~$0 | Captions API $0.15/min if bought ([Captions](https://captions.ai/help/docs/api/pricing)) |
| Composition | Remotion or HyperFrames + FFmpeg | License/time | Programmatic b-roll, punch-ins, captions ([HyperFrames](https://github.com/heygen-com/hyperframes)) |

The cost math favors the format that also looks best. Creators hide avatar tells (eyes, teeth, mouth blur, stiff hands) by showing the face for only **3–4 seconds at a time** and cutting to b-roll, screenshots and captions ([Stormy AI](https://stormy.ai/blog/ai-avatar-short-form-video-rowan-cheung-playbook)). A 35-second short with about 40% avatar screen time needs about 14 rendered seconds. That's **about $1.13 on Avatar IV or $1.68 on Avatar V**, plus about $0.05 of voice. A full talking-head render of the same short runs $2.82–4.20. At 20 shorts a month, avatar spend is roughly $25–85. It is cheap enough that cost shouldn't drive any decision. Quality per second should.

Self-hosted open models aren't worth it as the primary path. InfiniteTalk needs 28 GB+ of VRAM and reportedly about 16 minutes per 40 seconds of output. LatentSync and MuseTalk show identity drift, teeth artifacts and mouth blur ([Instavar](https://instavar.com/research/ai-video/open-source-lip-sync-models); [Atlas Cloud](https://www.atlascloud.ai/infinitetalk)). The one exception is valuable: running LatentSync or Sync.so over Brian's *real* existing footage with new audio keeps real gestures and backgrounds. That makes it the most convincing option for a creator with a large back catalog. Treat it as the Phase 3 upgrade.

## A trend engine is a median-baseline outlier detector plus an LLM

The official APIs won't do niche-wide discovery. The TikTok Research API bars commercial use ([TikTok Research API FAQ](https://developers.tiktok.com/docs/en/research-api-faq)). The Display API returns only the authorized user's own videos ([TikTok docs](https://developers.tiktok.com/docs/en/display-api-get-started)). Instagram hashtag search is capped at 30 hashtags per rolling 7 days ([Meta docs](https://developers.facebook.com/documentation/instagram-platform/instagram-api-with-facebook-login/hashtag-search)). Only the YouTube Data API is usable, within 10,000 units/day ([ChannelCrawler](https://channelcrawler.com/insights/youtube-api-daily-limit-quotas-costs-and-how-to-scale-beyond-10000-units-channelcrawler)). The practical route is third-party APIs at about **$1–2 per 1,000 records**: ScrapeCreators at $47 for 25k credits, EnsembleData from $100/month, and Apify's TikTok scraper at about $1.70 per 1,000 results. Apify actors also cover TikTok Creative Center trend lists ([SocialCrawl](https://www.socialcrawl.dev/blog/best-tiktok-data-apis-2026); [Apify](https://apify.com/datapeak/tiktok-creative-center)). Tracking 50–200 accounts in AI-for-developers, coding education and dev careers costs well under $100/month.

Sandcastles, vidIQ and Subscribr all share a core algorithm, and it isn't secret. They score a video's views against its own channel's baseline, so a 10x outlier means 10x that channel's normal performance ([vidIQ](https://support.vidiq.com/en/articles/9660010); [Sandcastles via GTM Directory](https://thegtmdirectory.com/tools/sandcastles)). Use the median of the account's prior posts, not the mean, because one past hit inflates the average ([ytgrowth](https://ytgrowth.io/blog/youtube-outlier-video)). Add a velocity score (views per hour since posting) ([Subscribr](https://help.subscribr.ai/article/48-what-are-velocity-and-outlier-scores)), a views-per-follower score to catch breakout small accounts, an age filter of 48–72 hours, and a sends-per-view weight. Mosseri names sends as a main signal for reaching non-followers ([Social Media Today](https://www.socialmediatoday.com/news/instagram-shares-algorithm-insights-2025/738034/)). Flag at 3x and prioritize 5–10x. These thresholds are common practice, not documented constants. Then transcribe the flagged videos and have the LLM extract a structured record: hook text and type, first-frame visual, format, beat structure, payoff timestamp, length and CTA. Cluster the records into a format library. **Promote a format only when it recurs as an outlier across several accounts.** That filters out lucky topics. Copy structure, never scripts. Copied scripts are a copyright problem and a voice problem.

The formats with the best evidence for a developer-education brand have clear patterns. Payoff arrives inside 15 seconds. Length is 20–40 seconds. There is a visual change about every 4 seconds, and captions are burned in. One vendor analysis reports 62% completion at 21–34 seconds versus 48% past 60 seconds, 58% versus 41% retention with pattern interrupts, and 4.3x impressions at 70% versus 40% watch time ([OpusClip](https://www.opus.pro/blog/tiktok-length-format-retention-data)). Those numbers come with no methodology, so treat them as direction, not law. In B2B content, screen recordings are "enormously watchable" and rarely done well ([BlitzCut](https://blitzcutai.com/blog/b2b-saas-reel-hooks-2026)), and founder-confession and one-feature demo clips work ([mean.ceo](https://blog.mean.ceo/?p=6927)). For Brian, that points to six formats: "I built X with Claude in N minutes" screen recordings, contrarian job-market takes, AI-hype myth-busts, numbered tool tricks, green-screen reacts to AI news, and founder mistakes. On posting cadence, Buffer's 11.4M-post TikTok dataset shows +17% views per post at 2–5 posts/week versus weekly, rising to +29% at 6–10 ([Buffer](https://buffer.com/resources/how-often-should-you-post-on-tiktok/)). Start at 3–5 per platform per week.

Brian has an advantage Doxa's clients lack. Scripts should come from RAG over his own corpus of articles, LinkedIn posts and video transcripts, with the format library acting only as the structural template. Doxa has to manufacture a voice from a 5–10 minute questionnaire. Brian already has years of opinions on record. That is the difference between "AI clone saying generic things" and "Brian's actual takes, rendered faster."

## Build the pipeline, buy the engines, film once and keep filming

The recommendation is to build. Don't buy Doxa: it is a Portuguese-first, high-ticket service whose guarantee optimizes raw views, not developer leads. Don't run REA. Write a thin orchestration layer in TypeScript or Python over vendor APIs, with a human approval gate before anything is posted.

| Stage | Build or buy | Implementation |
|---|---|---|
| Trend ingest | Buy data, build logic | ScrapeCreators/EnsembleData + YouTube Data API → Postgres; nightly job |
| Outlier scoring | Build | `views / median(prior N)`, velocity, views/follower, sends/view, 48–72h age gate |
| Format extraction | Build | Whisper transcription → LLM JSON schema → clustering → format library |
| Scripting | Build | RAG over Brian's corpus + format template → 5–10 hooks → Brian approves |
| Voice | Buy | ElevenLabs PVC (Eleven v3) with alignment timestamps |
| Avatar | Buy | HeyGen v3 `POST /v3/videos` with uploaded audio; IV default, V for hero clips |
| Composition | Build | Remotion/HyperFrames: word-highlight captions, screen recordings, b-roll, punch-ins every 3–4s, avatar in short bursts |
| Publish + learn | Build, manual first | Human review, AI-label toggles on; pull own metrics back into the scorer |

Brian's filming requirement is small. One session of about 30 minutes covers 3–4 HeyGen looks of about 2 minutes each (different shirts and backgrounds, desk-level mid-shot framing that hides weak hand motion) plus the consent recording. One to three hours of existing long-form audio trains the voice. After that, his ongoing input is approving scripts, recording the screen captures he already makes for teaching, and filming one or two real-face videos a week for stories and opinions.

The phases are gated by evidence, not effort. **Phase 0, week 1:** with no code, make ten shorts by hand in the HeyGen web app and ElevenLabs, post them labeled, and compare 3-second hold rate, average watch time and sends against Brian's last ten real-face shorts. **Phase 1, weeks 2–4:** build a CLI that turns a topic into script, voice, avatar segments, Remotion composition and a 1080x1920 MP4. Target under 15 minutes of Brian's time per video. **Phase 2, weeks 4–8:** add the trend engine and format library, and switch topic selection from intuition to outliers. **Phase 3, month 3 onward:** feed his own post metrics back into format weighting, A/B test hooks, and try LatentSync or Sync.so re-dubs of real footage. The kill criterion: if clone shorts hold below roughly 80% of his real-face average watch time after 20 posts, keep the trend and scripting engine and record the scripts himself. That part of the system is valuable whatever the avatar does. All-in cost is about $1–4 per video in render and voice, plus $50–100/month of data, LLM spend in cents per script, and whatever Remotion's company license costs. Verify that license before committing to Remotion.

The risks deserve as much weight as the build. **Trust:** a preregistered 2026 study (n=2,000) found AI-mediated video reduces interpersonal trust ([arXiv 2603.18868](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.18868)). Viewers who learn about an avatar upfront mostly react positively, while undisclosed avatars draw "overwhelmingly negative" reactions once discovered ([Training Magazine](https://trainingmag.com/leveraging-ai-avatars-and-voices-for-scalable-trusted-learning/)). Brian's buyers are paying for his judgment, so getting caught with an undisclosed clone would cost far more than any views it earns. **Labeling:** TikTok requires labels on realistic AI people and voices and detects C2PA credentials automatically ([Storrito](https://storrito.com/resources/tiktoks-2026-ai-labeling-rules-and-what-they-signal-for-platform-governance/)). It says the label itself doesn't reduce distribution ([jackrighteous](https://jackrighteous.com/blogs/ai-art-visuals-creatives/tiktok-ai-music-video-labels-spam-reach-2026)). Instagram reportedly limits reach for unlabeled AI-person profiles ([postora](https://www.postora.org/blog/ai-generated-content-labels-social-media-2026)), and EU AI Act transparency duties took effect August 2, 2026 ([weventure](https://weventure.de/en/blog/ai-labeling)). Always label. Framing "my AI clone explains…" as a feature fits a brand that teaches AI. **Spam detection:** TikTok is testing detection of accounts dedicated to AI spam ([jackrighteous](https://jackrighteous.com/blogs/ai-art-visuals-creatives/tiktok-ai-music-video-labels-spam-reach-2026)), so mix formats and real footage instead of posting a uniform templated feed. **ToS:** *Meta v. Bright Data* protects logged-off scraping of public data only at the district-court level and only under Meta's terms ([Quinn Emanuel](https://www.quinnemanuel.com/the-firm/news-events/client-alert-what-does-the-meta-v-bright-data-summary-judgment-ruling-mean-for-web-scraping/)). Pull metadata through vendors, never scrape while logged in, and use downloaded competitor video only for internal transcription.

## Conclusion

Doxa turns out to be a sales machine on top of HeyGen and ElevenLabs, not a technology company. For a solo creator, the defensible asset was never the avatar. It is the loop that finds proven formats and fills them with an authentic point of view, and Brian already owns the hardest input to that loop: a large, searchable corpus of his own opinions. That flips the usual framing. The trend-to-script engine is the durable investment, and the clone is just one swappable renderer behind it, alongside his real face and future lip-sync re-dubs.

There's a second payoff. A RAG-driven, outlier-scored, avatar-rendered content pipeline is exactly the kind of practical AI system Parsity teaches. Built in public with honest labeling and honest retention numbers, the system becomes curriculum and content in its own right. That framing turns the biggest risk, an audience discovering an AI clone, into the hook itself.
